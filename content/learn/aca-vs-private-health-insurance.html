When people shop for their own health insurance, they usually run into two kinds of plans: plans that follow the Affordable Care Act (ACA) and plans that don’t. Both are often called “private” insurance, since private companies sell them, but the rules behind them are very different. Here’s how they compare and how to decide.

What makes a plan “ACA-compliant”

ACA-compliant plans are sold on the Health Insurance Marketplace (HealthCare.gov or your state’s exchange) and also directly from insurers “off-exchange.” Either way, they must follow the same core rules:

  • Guaranteed issue: you can’t be turned down because of your health.
  • No pre-existing condition exclusions, and no higher price because of your health history. Prices can vary only by age, location, tobacco use, plan and who’s covered.
  • Ten essential health benefits, including hospital care, emergency services, maternity and newborn care, mental health and substance use treatment, prescription drugs, preventive care and pediatric dental and vision.
  • No annual or lifetime dollar limits on essential health benefits, and an annual cap on your in-network out-of-pocket costs.

Only plans bought through the Marketplace are eligible for premium tax credits and cost-sharing reductions. See ACA Marketplace health insurance.

What “non-ACA” private plans are

Several kinds of coverage are regulated differently and don’t have to meet all of the ACA’s rules:

  • Medically underwritten health plans, which can price or approve coverage based on your health history.
  • Short-term health insurance, meant to fill a temporary gap. Federal and state rules limit how long these plans can last.
  • Fixed indemnity plans, which pay a set dollar amount per day, visit or service rather than paying a share of the actual bill.
  • Health care sharing ministries, which aren’t insurance at all and don’t guarantee that bills will be paid.

These products can cost less for some healthy people, and some offer large provider networks. The trade-off is that they may exclude pre-existing conditions, cap benefits or leave out services such as maternity care, mental health care or prescriptions. How to decide

Start with your income

If your household income qualifies you for Marketplace savings, a subsidized ACA plan is often the best value, especially on a Silver plan if you qualify for cost-sharing reductions. For 2026 and later, premium tax credits are generally available to households with incomes from 100% to 400% of the federal poverty level.

Then look at your health

If you or someone in your family has an ongoing condition, takes regular medication or is planning a pregnancy, the protections of an ACA plan matter a lot. A plan that excludes a condition you already have can leave you paying the full cost of care for it.

Then check your doctors and timing

Make sure the doctors and hospitals you use are in-network (see PPO vs. HMO vs. EPO). And remember timing: ACA plans have enrollment windows (see the open enrollment guide), while many non-ACA plans can be bought any time if you’re approved.
